# Suresh content evaluation standard

**Purpose:** Evaluate Collate content before publication across web, email, social, campaigns, sales assets, and launch content.

**Source:** `Messaging Notes from Suresh.md`

**North star:** The reader should recognize the problem immediately, understand every sentence on the first read, and reach the next action without encountering unsupported claims, internal language, disconnected ideas, or formulaic AI writing.

## Passing standard

Content is publication-ready only when:

- Every dimension scores **10/10**.
- Every hard gate passes.
- Every factual claim has been checked against the current source of truth.
- The evaluator can cite exact language from the draft as evidence for each score.

Do not average the scores. A 10 in one dimension cannot compensate for a 7 in another. Report the score as a vector, for example: `10 / 9 / 10 / 8 / 10 / 9 / 10 / 10`.

## Hard gates

If any answer is **yes**, the content cannot receive a perfect score and must be revised before publication.

1. Does the draft use a number, customer result, performance claim, comparison, superlative, or scale claim without a traceable source and defensible calculation?
2. Does the draft use an internal product or feature name without a plain-language explanation on first mention?
3. Does the opening lead with Collate, a product feature, or a category abstraction when the format allows it to lead with a recognizable customer problem?
4. Does any paragraph introduce an idea that is not connected to the problem, argument, or action before or after it?
5. Does the draft contain obvious AI-writing patterns that an editor has not resolved?
6. Does the draft reflect retired, inconsistent, or unapproved positioning?
7. Does the content ask for more attention than its usefulness justifies?

## Scoring scale

Use the following anchors for every dimension:

- **10:** Fully satisfies the rule. Evidence is visible in the draft. No meaningful revision remains.
- **8–9:** Strong, but at least one phrase, transition, claim, or structural choice still weakens the rule.
- **6–7:** Partially satisfies the rule. The intended idea is present, but the reader must work or trust the writer too much.
- **3–5:** The rule is addressed inconsistently or superficially. Material rewriting is required.
- **0–2:** The rule is absent, contradicted, or creates publication risk.

A score of 10 is not “good for a draft.” It means ready to ship against that dimension.

## The eight dimensions

### 1. Recognizable problem first

**Question:** Does the content begin with a problem the intended reader already recognizes in language they would use?

**10/10 requires:**

- The first substantive line names a concrete problem, cost, risk, or blocked outcome.
- The problem is specific to the target segment and initiative state.
- The reader can recognize themselves without first understanding Collate terminology.
- The opening creates a logical need for the solution that follows.

**Deductions:**

- Starts with a category claim, company description, product announcement, or feature list.
- Uses a generic problem such as “data is complex” or “AI is changing everything.”
- Assumes every reader has an AI use case when the target segment may not.

### 2. Simple, concrete language

**Question:** Can the intended reader understand every sentence on the first pass?

**10/10 requires:**

- Concrete nouns and active verbs do most of the work.
- Necessary technical terms are familiar to the persona or explained in place.
- Abstract terms such as context, ontology, governance, intelligence, orchestration, and semantics are tied to observable data, actions, or outcomes.
- No sentence needs to be reread to determine its subject or claim.

**Deductions:**

- Stacked abstractions, noun chains, vague verbs, or category jargon.
- Claims such as “activate context” or “enable trusted AI” without explaining what changes.
- Long sentences that contain multiple independent promises.

### 3. External language, not internal names

**Question:** Does the copy explain the value before relying on Collate product names?

**10/10 requires:**

- The first mention uses `Collate + product name` when a branded name is necessary.
- A plain-language explanation appears next to the name.
- The copy remains understandable if the reader has never seen the product UI.
- Feature names support the story instead of becoming the story.

**Deductions:**

- Unexplained names such as AI Studio, Context Center, Policy Agent, or Admin Analytics.
- Internal release language, roadmap shorthand, or organizational terminology in customer copy.
- A string of branded capabilities where a customer workflow would be clearer.

### 4. Connected argument

**Question:** Does each idea earn the next one and tie back to the opening problem?

**10/10 requires:**

- The logic can be summarized as `problem → consequence → required change → Collate approach → proof → action`.
- Every paragraph has one job.
- Transitions explain why the next idea matters.
- Examples and features resolve the problem that opened the piece.
- The CTA is the natural next step, not an abrupt sales request.

**Deductions:**

- Topic jumps, parallel feature lists, or a conclusion that introduces a new idea.
- A feature appears because it is available, not because it advances the argument.
- The opening promises one subject while the body discusses another.

### 5. Provable numbers and claims

**Question:** Can Collate show the source and, when relevant, the math behind every material claim?

**10/10 requires:**

- Every number has a named, retrievable source.
- Derived figures include the inputs, assumptions, time period, and calculation.
- Customer outcomes are attributed and scoped to what that customer actually used.
- Benchmarks identify the dataset, model, baseline, intervention, and measurement.
- Qualifiers match the evidence: GA, preview, directional, observed, or planned.

**Deductions:**

- Unsourced percentages, savings, time reductions, accuracy lifts, adoption counts, or market claims.
- “Only,” “best,” “leading,” “complete,” or similar claims without comparative evidence.
- A correct source used to imply a broader conclusion than it supports.

### 6. Human-edited voice

**Question:** Does the content sound deliberately written rather than generated from a messaging template?

**10/10 requires:**

- Sentence lengths and structures vary naturally.
- The draft uses the fewest rhetorical devices needed.
- Short fragments, triads, mirrored constructions, and slogan patterns appear only when they improve understanding.
- No long sentence circles through abstractions without landing on a concrete point.
- A human editor has removed repetition, filler, and synthetic transitions.

**Common AI tells to inspect:**

- Repeated three-part constructions.
- “It’s not X. It’s Y.” used as a reflex.
- “From X to Y,” “not just X, but Y,” and repeated imperative headings.
- Excessive em dashes, throat-clearing, symmetrical bullets, and generic intensifiers.
- Several consecutive sections with identical cadence.

### 7. Respect for the reader’s time

**Question:** Does every sentence repay the attention it asks for?

**10/10 requires:**

- The format is only as long as the reader and decision require.
- Every sentence adds understanding, evidence, differentiation, or useful direction.
- The main point and next action are easy to find by scanning.
- Repetition is used only to aid navigation or conversion.
- The content answers the reader’s likely question before adding company context.

**Deductions:**

- Repeated claims, ornamental setup, unnecessary history, or exhaustive feature coverage.
- Multiple equal CTAs.
- Long copy used to compensate for an unclear central idea.

### 8. Current and consistent quality bar

**Question:** Is the content aligned with the current approved positioning, audience, product availability, and surrounding campaign?

**10/10 requires:**

- The target segment and relationship are explicit: OpenMetadata user, Collate customer, prospect, practitioner, buyer, or another defined audience.
- OpenMetadata and Collate are clearly distinguished when relevant.
- Terminology matches the latest messaging source of truth.
- Product claims match current GA, preview, and roadmap status.
- Related assets in the same journey do not contradict the message.
- The draft has completed factual, editorial, and stakeholder review appropriate to its risk.

**Deductions:**

- Mixed audience or lifecycle stage.
- Retired sub-brand, old category language, or inconsistent product naming.
- Launch copy applied to evergreen content without updating availability language.
- One asset changes positioning while connected nurture, landing, or sales content remains inconsistent.

### D. Revision plan

List issues in this order:

1. Trust and factual risk.
2. Audience or problem mismatch.
3. Broken argument or missing connection.
4. Abstraction and terminology.
5. Length, cadence, and polish.

For each issue, provide:

- The exact language causing the issue.
- Why it fails for this reader.
- A specific revision instruction.
- A proposed replacement when the correction does not require missing evidence or a stakeholder decision.

### E. Revised version and re-score

When asked to improve the content:

1. Revise only after completing the first scorecard.
2. Preserve supported facts and the intended action.
3. Re-run all hard gates against the revision.
4. Re-score all eight dimensions from scratch.
5. Do not claim 10/10 if a source, product-status decision, audience choice, or proof point remains unresolved. Mark the blocker explicitly.

## Evaluator prompt

```text
Act as a demanding editorial evaluator for Collate. Evaluate the supplied content against the Suresh content evaluation standard.

Do not reward polish that hides a weak problem, disconnected logic, internal terminology, unsupported claims, AI-writing patterns, wasted words, or outdated positioning. Do not average scores. A publication-ready result requires 10/10 in every dimension and a pass on every hard gate.

First establish the asset type, target reader, initiative state, relationship to Collate/OpenMetadata, intended action, and current source material. Then:

1. Run every hard gate.
2. Score all eight dimensions using the published anchors.
3. Quote evidence from the draft for every score.
4. Identify exactly what prevents a 10.
5. Prioritize revisions by factual risk, audience fit, logic, clarity, then polish.
6. Produce a revised version only when requested.
7. Re-run the complete evaluation after revision.

Never invent evidence, product availability, audience insight, or customer proof to improve a score. If information is missing, state the blocker and the decision or source required.
```

## Drafting checklist

Before writing:

1. Define the reader, relationship, initiative state, problem, and action.
2. Select the current source-of-truth files.
3. Decide what one argument the asset will make.
4. Inventory every proposed number and proof source.

While writing:

1. Open with the reader’s problem.
2. Use plain language before product language.
3. Make each paragraph answer the question created by the previous one.
4. Include only features needed to resolve the problem.
5. Keep one primary action.

Before publishing:

1. Run the hard gates and eight-part scorecard.
2. Verify every number and availability claim.
3. Read aloud for cadence and AI tells.
4. Cut anything that does not repay attention.
5. Confirm connected campaign assets use the same current positioning.
